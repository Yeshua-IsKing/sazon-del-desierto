Los Sazones

The seasoning DNA of two regions

These neighboring traditions sit at opposite ends of the spice spectrum. Knowing the difference keeps any blend — or any plate — true to where it comes from.

Sonora

Austere, meat-forward

"Good beef needs very little dressing up."

The hot, dry, historically isolated north pushed cooks toward simplicity. The vast majority of dishes lean on only a small handful of seasonings — and let quality and fire do the rest.

Core pantry

  • Sea / kosher salt
  • Black pepper
  • Garlic
  • Onion
  • Cumin
  • Mexican oregano
  • Bay leaf
  • Cilantro & coriander seed
  • Chile colorado

Chiltepín — the tiny wild chile called el oro rojo (red gold), ~100,000–200,000 SHU. Crushed into menudo, pozole & gallina pinta, and the base of countless table salsas.

Mesquite smoke is itself a seasoning — carne asada is grilled over mesquite coals for exactly that aroma.

Baja California

Coastal, layered, fusion

Mexican roots, Mediterranean & Asian layers.

Seafood-forward and cosmopolitan, especially in the north — Tijuana, Ensenada, Mexicali and the Valle de Guadalupe — where the modern Baja Med movement braids three culinary worlds together.

Shared Mexican base

  • Lime
  • Cilantro
  • Garlic & onion
  • Oregano (incl. wild)
  • Cumin
  • Epazote
  • Serrano · chile de árbol

The fusion layers

  • Olive oil & olives
  • Rosemary · thyme · basil
  • Wine
  • Soy & ginger
  • Lemongrass

Damiana — a wild native herb of Baja California Sur, used as a seasoning and famous as the base of a local sweet liqueur.

Las Salsas

What lives on the Sonoran table

Two essentials carry most plates — the fiery wild-chile salsa and the smoky roasted salsa built for carne asada — plus the everyday shortcuts.

Salsa de Chiltepín

Makes ~1½ cups

The essential Sonoran salsa.

Ingredients

  • 2 ripe tomatoes (roma or bola)
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • ½–1 tsp dried chiltepín (≈7–10 chiles), lightly toasted
  • 1 tsp apple-cider or white vinegar
  • ¼ tsp Mexican oregano (optional)
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Roast tomatoes (and garlic) on a dry comal until the skins blister; peel.
  2. Toast the chiltepines briefly — never handle them with bare hands; use a spoon or towel.
  3. Blend everything ~2 minutes until smooth, or grind by hand in a molcajete with ¼ onion for a coarser salsa.
Heat note: chiltepín is intensely hot — add a few, taste, build up. Keeps refrigerated 1–2 weeks. Tip: keep a chiltepinero, a mortar used only for grinding chiltepín.

Salsa Asada

Makes ~1½ cups

The roasted carne-asada companion.

Ingredients

  • 4 roma tomatoes
  • 2–4 serrano chiles (jalapeño for milder)
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • ¼ onion (optional)
  • Small handful of cilantro
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Char tomatoes, serranos, garlic (and onion) on a comal or grill until blistered and soft.
  2. Blend — or grind in a molcajete — with cilantro and salt to a coarse texture.
  3. Adjust salt and spoon over carne asada and tacos.
Storage: refrigerated, up to 5 days. The char is what makes it — don't skip blistering the skins.

Las Recetas

What Sonora does best

Five signature dishes — from the weekend grill to the dessert that closes it out. Tap a dish to open its recipe. Measurements are imperial; allergens are flagged.

No. 1 · The centerpiece

Carne Asada, Estilo Sonora

The cultural heart of the state — a weekend ritual built on top-quality beef and mesquite smoke. The approach is famously minimalist: with good meat, salt is often the only seasoning.

Serves4–6
Dish heat
ContainsGluten

For the grill

  • 2 lb thin-cut beef — ribeye, top sirloin, NY strip, or arrachera (skirt/flank)
  • Coarse kosher or sea salt (essential — often the only seasoning)
  • Black pepper (optional)

Optional norteño marinade

  • Juice of 2–3 limes (or 1 sour orange)
  • 2–3 garlic cloves, minced
  • ¼ cup light beer
  • 1 tbsp Worcestershire (salsa inglesa)

Method

  1. Bring the meat to room temperature. Salt generously just before grilling.
  2. Build a mesquite fire (or charcoal with a chunk of mesquite or oak). Wait until the flames die down to glowing coals — you cook on the heat, not the flame.
  3. Grill over high heat, flipping only once, to medium. Don't overcook.
  4. Rest a few minutes, then slice or chop.
  5. If marinating: coat the meat, refrigerate 1–2 hrs (up to overnight), drain, then salt and grill as above.
Serve with: warm flour tortillas, salsa asada, grilled spring onions (cebollitas), guacamole, lime and beans. With top-quality beef, salt + mesquite smoke is the entire point — resist over-seasoning.
No. 2 · The breakfast

Machaca con Huevo

The quintessential Sonoran breakfast — dried, shredded beef scrambled with eggs. The drying-and-pounding technique was developed by desert peoples to preserve beef in extreme heat, then carried by 19th-century vaqueros as portable food.

Serves4
Dish heat
ContainsEggGluten

Ingredients

  • 8 oz machaca / dried shredded beef (≈1½ cups)
  • 4 large eggs, lightly beaten
  • ½ medium white onion, finely chopped
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 2 small tomatoes, diced
  • 1–2 serrano chiles, finely chopped (optional)
  • 1–2 tbsp vegetable oil
  • Salt to taste — add cautiously; machaca is already salty

Method

  1. If using packaged dry machaca, shred it fine. If very dry, soak 5 minutes in warm water and drain well.
  2. Heat oil over medium-high. Sauté onion and garlic ~2 minutes until translucent.
  3. Add the machaca; cook 3–4 minutes until lightly browned and aromatic.
  4. Add tomato and serrano; cook 2–3 minutes until the tomato softens.
  5. Pour in the beaten eggs and stir gently until just set — don't let it dry out.
  6. Taste, and add salt only if needed.
Serve with: warm flour tortillas burrito-style, refried beans and red salsa. Low-sodium tip: rinse the machaca briefly and skip added salt entirely.
No. 3 · The regional pride

Chimichanga

A fried burrito invented in Sonora in the 1950s. A large flour tortilla is stuffed, folded into a rectangular packet, and fried until golden and crisp.

Makes4
Dish heat
ContainsGlutenDairy

Filling

  • 1 lb beef chuck or flank, cooked & shredded (leftover beef or machaca works)
  • 1 tbsp oil
  • ¼ onion, chopped; 1 garlic clove; 1 small tomato; 1 chile (serrano/Anaheim)
  • Salt & pepper to taste

Assembly

  • 4 large flour tortillas (burrito size)
  • 1 cup refried beans (optional)
  • 1 cup shredded cheese — Chihuahua, Monterey Jack, or queso
  • Oil for frying
  • Toppings: shredded lettuce, salsa, crumbled queso, crema, guacamole

Method

  1. Sauté onion, garlic, tomato and chile; add the shredded beef, season, and warm through.
  2. Warm the tortillas so they fold without cracking.
  3. Spread beans down the center, add beef and cheese, then fold into a sealed rectangular packet (fold the sides in, then roll). A toothpick holds the seam.
  4. Fry in ~1 inch of hot oil (350°F / 175°C), seam-side down first, turning, until golden and crisp — 2–3 minutes. Drain.
  5. Top with lettuce, salsa, cheese and crema. Serve immediately.
Lighter option: brush with oil and bake at 425°F (220°C) until golden instead of frying.
No. 4 · The everyday comfort

Caldo de Queso

A humble, beloved Hermosillo soup — potatoes, roasted green chile and fresh cheese in a light tomato-and-onion broth. The dish that most often repeats on Sonoran home tables, and named by Taste Atlas among the world's best soups.

Serves6
Dish heat
ContainsDairy

Ingredients

  • 1½ lb (680 g) potatoes, peeled, ½-inch cubes
  • 4–6 Anaheim / California green chiles, roasted, peeled, seeded, sliced
  • 2 small tomatoes (~1 cup), diced
  • ⅓ cup white onion, finely chopped
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 6–7 cups chicken or vegetable broth (to cover potatoes by ~1 in)
  • ½ cup (120 ml) whole milk
  • 1 tsp Mexican oregano (up to 1 tbsp)
  • 1–2 tbsp oil
  • 8 oz (225 g) queso fresco, ranchero or cotija, ½-inch cubes
  • Salt & pepper to taste
  • To serve: crushed chiltepín, flour tortillas, avocado

Method

  1. Roast the chiles until blistered, steam in a covered bowl 10–15 min, then peel, seed and slice.
  2. Sauté onion, garlic and tomato in the oil to form a light sofrito.
  3. Add the broth and bring to a boil. Add potatoes; simmer 15–20 min until tender but holding shape.
  4. Stir in the chile strips and oregano; lower the heat.
  5. Add the milk slowly, below a rolling boil — adding milk to boiling broth will curdle it.
  6. Adjust salt and pepper. Off the heat, add the cheese cubes; let sit 1–2 min to soften without fully melting.
Finish each bowl with crushed chiltepín. Low-sodium tip: use unsalted homemade broth, skip the bouillon, and let the cheese provide the savor. Vegetarian as written with vegetable broth.
No. 5 · The sweet finish

Coyotas

Sonora's signature dessert — a flat, golden, lightly sweet wheat pastry filled with piloncillo. It originated in Villa de Seris, Hermosillo, where the Ochoa family began selling them formally in 1953; the name nods to the coyote-gold color they take on as they bake.

Makes12–14
Dish heat
ContainsGlutenEgg

Dough

  • 4 cups (500 g) all-purpose flour
  • 1 cup (225 g) vegetable shortening or lard, room temp
  • ½ tbsp baking powder
  • ½ tsp salt
  • Syrup: ¾ cup grated piloncillo (150 g) + ½ cup (120 ml) water

Filling & finish

  • ¾ cup grated piloncillo (150 g) + 1 tbsp flour
  • 1 egg (or egg white), beaten, for brushing
  • Granulated sugar, for sprinkling

Method

  1. Syrup: dissolve 150 g piloncillo in ½ cup water over low heat; reduce ~5 min to a light syrup; cool completely.
  2. Filling: toss 150 g piloncillo with 1 tbsp flour.
  3. Dough: combine flour, baking powder, salt; cut in shortening to a sandy texture (3–5 min). Add cooled syrup; work ~10 min to a smooth dough that pulls from your hands. Rest 30 min.
  4. Roll to ~¼ inch (5 mm); cut ~4-inch discs.
  5. Place ~1 tbsp filling in the center of half the discs. Brush edges with egg, top with a second disc, and seal the rim with a fork.
  6. Brush tops with egg, sprinkle sugar, and prick the center once with a fork (keeps them from puffing). Rest 30 min.
  7. Bake at 350°F (175°C) for 18–22 min, until evenly golden. Cool.
Serve with: the close of a carne asada, or alongside coffee — the piloncillo center means you don't need to sweeten your cup. Keep well-wrapped 5–7 days. Variations fill with cajeta, guava or quince.
Notas

Sourcing, storage & a word on sources

Sourcing & storage

  • Chiltepín — dried, at Mexican grocers & online; expensive because it's wild-harvested. A little goes a long way; store airtight, away from light.
  • Piloncillo — hard cones (one ≈ 8 oz / 225 g) or pre-grated. Microwave a few seconds to soften before chopping; dark brown sugar substitutes in a pinch.
  • Machaca / carne seca — Mexican markets & online; a brief warm-water soak revives bone-dry beef.
  • Mexican oregano — more citrusy and robust than Mediterranean oregano; worth seeking out.
  • General — whole dried spices keep ~1 year; ground spices & blends are best within 6 months. Airtight jars, cool and dark.

On sources

Recipes and seasoning profiles were assembled from Spanish-language regional and culinary sources — Sonoran outlets (El Sol de Hermosillo, Expreso, Visita Sonora, NORO), recipe sites (Cocina Delirante, El Universal, Animal Gourmet, Larousse Cocina, México en mi Cocina), and Baja / Baja Med references (San Diego Red, Club Valle de Guadalupe, Excélsior, POSTA) — and translated into English.

Where a dish's origin is disputed — machaca, for instance, is also claimed by Chihuahua and Nuevo León — it's noted as such. Measurements have been converted from metric to imperial throughout.
